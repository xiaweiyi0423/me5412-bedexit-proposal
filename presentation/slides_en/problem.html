Who it is for

  • Older adults at home who get up at night
  • Struggle to rise from a 0.42 m bed, but can stand with a higher seat and a handhold
  • Walk alone or with a walker; sit steadily, grip a bar, hold a switch
  • Not for: full-transfer patients; moderate–severe cognitive impairment

Design requirements (each verified at the end)

  1. Bed stays low, feet flat; rising effort as low as a raised bed
  2. Seat follows thighs, no forward slide; push ≤ about ½ body weight
  3. Lifts only with grip switch held + force check; release = stop
  4. Lift speed follows the user; personal settings
  5. Never tips: sitting on, shifting, rising, sitting back down
  6. Goes down only while "Down" is held; seat stays to catch you

The conflict: a low bed is hard to rise from, a high bed leaves the feet unsupported — a fixed bed height can't do both. STS model based on 60 kg, 1.60 m; structure checked for 100 kg × 1.5

Behind the film are three numbers. Of 12 older adults, half could not stand from a 38-centimetre bed without help. Fall-prevention advice says a bed should be no higher than about 0.58 metres; any higher, and the feet are not flat when seated. And about 22% of older adults at home have orthostatic hypotension, so they may feel dizzy after standing. So one fixed bed height cannot do both. Our users can still walk, alone or with a walker, but find it hard to rise from a normal bed. We will verify the six requirements on the right one by one at the end.
